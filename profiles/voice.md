# Voice and style for posts and profiles

How James sounds wherever he posts. Read this before drafting anything for LinkedIn, Instagram, GitHub or the blog,
and check the draft against it before showing it to him. The aim is a first draft that needs one pass.

The voice comes from the site's theme: the day passing, the craft that goes into the work, and the care for the
people who read it. The platforms differ in what they're for, and James sounds the same on all of them.

## The reference post

The October 2026 launch post took several rounds to get right. This is what went out, word for word. When a draft
feels off, read it next to this.

    For the last few years, my website has been trying to keep up with my trail. A project added here, a page
    dropped there, while new ideas kept arriving faster than I could give them a home.

    This week I let it catch its breath.

    Now one slow arc of light crosses the page every sixteen seconds, and each arc is a day. The elements catch it
    as it passes. The avatar rises and sets like a small sun. When night comes, the page fades like dusk, from warm
    paper and ink to moonlit blue and silver. All of it is anchored by the sundial that started the idea.

    At that pace, fourteen years go by in a single day.

    That is about how it feels when I look back at everything I've built. Years of work, and I can watch all of it
    pass before dinner.

    Come and watch a few days go by with me: codesthings.com

Notes on the post, and on what the drafts before it got wrong:

- **It opens with a story, not a hook.** The first lines say what was going on and why. Nothing is held back to make
  people click "see more".
- **James is the one doing things.** "I let it catch its breath", "everything I've built". The early drafts said
  things happened to the site; the post says he made them happen.
- **It describes the work by what you'd see.** The arc, the avatar rising and setting, the fade at dusk. It doesn't
  call any of it beautiful or innovative.
- **A short line on its own marks each turn.** "This week I let it catch its breath." and "At that pace, fourteen
  years go by in a single day." Two in the whole post.
- **There's one real figure, and it carries the meaning.** Sixteen seconds per day makes fourteen years about a day.
  The number is the idea.
- **It closes on an invitation, not a request.** "Come and watch a few days go by with me." It doesn't ask for
  reactions or shares or a job.
- **Two things were cut from the drafts.** The CV pitch and the "open to senior or principal roles" line both went.
  The profile already says both, and the post read better as a moment than as an advert. A role ask can go in a post
  now and then, never in every one.

## The voice everywhere

These hold on every platform, in every post, profile and README.

- **Made with care.** Show the thing, say how it was made, give real numbers where they exist. Let the work speak and
  keep the adjectives out of its way.
- **Thoughtful.** Time passing, looking back, what something taught him. Reflective, and never preachy.
- **Friendly and easy to approach.** Written like he'd say it to someone across a table. Warm, a little dry, and he
  doesn't take himself too seriously.
- **Caring about the people who read it.** Write for the reader's benefit: a pitfall saved, a file to download, a
  guide for the next person. Credit others by name when they helped.
- **Quietly confident.** Active verbs and plain claims: "I built", "I rewrote", "it now handles". No hedging ("I was
  lucky enough to", "I tried to"), and no chest-beating ("game-changing", "proud to announce").
- **Honest about failure.** The dead ends and wrong turns are part of the story, told plainly and without
  self-deprecation for effect.

### Platforms don't change the voice

LinkedIn, Instagram and the rest change their formats and boost paid accounts whatever anyone writes. The writing
stays the same for every format.

- A strong first line is welcome when it comes out of the story. Never write a line just to get the "see more" click,
  hold back the point, or open with a question to bait replies.
- No engagement endings: "Thoughts?", "Agree?", "Drop a comment", "Tag someone who".
- No formats borrowed from the feed: lines of single sentences, numbered "lessons", emoji used as bullet points, "Here's
  what I learned" set-ups.
- Write for whoever finds the post a year from now as much as for the feed this week.

## Style

How the writing looks on the page. These apply everywhere unless a platform section below says otherwise.

- **British English, plain words.** Use, not utilise. Help, not empower. Built, not leveraged.
- **Short paragraphs.** One to three sentences. A one-line paragraph marks a turn in the story, at most twice a post.
- **No em dashes.** Use a full stop or a comma. Hyphens in compound words are fine.
- **Facts from the source.** Figures, dates and claims about past work come from `cv/data/*.json`, the repos, or a
  photo's own metadata, never from memory. If a fact can't be found, leave it out.
- **Numbers as figures for measurements** (1M+, 16s, f/2), words where the number is part of the story ("fourteen
  years", "sixteen seconds").
- **Name the specific thing.** "A Helios 44 on a Micro Four Thirds body", not "a vintage lens". "The CampSnap Pro", not
  "a camera".
- **Emoji:** none on LinkedIn or GitHub. On Instagram, at most one, and only if James would send it in a message.
- **Hashtags:** none on LinkedIn. On Instagram a few specific ones that photographers and makers actually follow
  (`#campsnap`, `#helios44`, `#infrared`), never generic ones (`#photography`, `#instagood`).
- **Links:** in the body. One per post where possible.

### Words and phrases that don't appear

- "Excited to announce", "thrilled", "humbled", "proud to share", "game-changer", "passionate about".
- "Journey" for a career, "leverage", "synergy", "deep dive", "unlock", "elevate", "seamless", "robust" as praise.
- "In today's fast-paced world", "Here's the thing", "Let that sink in", "This.", "Read that again".
- "Not X, but Y" constructions set up for effect.
- Lists of three where two or four is true.

## LinkedIn: the craftsman telling a story

**What it's for.** Showing the work and the person behind it, to people who might work with James. A professional
account, but one that sounds like him: a story about something made, how it came together, and what it meant.

**What goes on it.** Things built, at work or for fun, and what they taught him. Engineering stories with real numbers
and no confidential detail. The maker and photography side now and then, because that's part of who he is. The
schedule (`linkedin/schedule.md`) holds the order.

**How a post is built.**

1. **Open with the story.** What was going on, in one to three sentences.
2. **The turn.** A short line where something changed.
3. **The work.** What he made or did, described as you'd see or use it, with the one figure that matters.
4. **What it means.** One or two sentences of reflection. Not a lesson for the reader.
5. **The invitation.** Where to see it, in words he'd actually say.

**Length.** 100 to 200 words. If it needs more, it's a blog post, and the LinkedIn post points to it.

**Media.** Every post has media: screenshots, photos, a short recording, or a diagram drawn in the site's style. Alt
text for every image.

**Roles.** The profile carries "open to senior or principal remote roles". A post mentions it only when the post is
about looking for work.

**Example: something made (schedule slot 5)**

    Air65 whoops are tiny, and so is the margin for error when one meets a tree. [One sentence on what kept going
    wrong with the stock canopy.] So I started modelling my own.

    The Dome took two revisions.

    [What the first version got wrong, and what the second changed, in two sentences, described as you'd see it in
    the hand.] Other pilots have printed it since. It sits at 4.9 from 43 reviews, and their photos of it in the wild
    are the best part.

    It's free on MakerWorld: [link]

The brackets are facts to fill from the MakerWorld listing or from James. The review figure comes from
`base-website/data/projects/dome-whoop-canopy.json` and needs rechecking on the day.

**Example: engineering (schedule slot 6)**

    The release ingestion pipeline was handling around 250,000 releases a day. Then the managed Kafka provider shut
    down, and every alternative cost twice as much or more.

    So we moved away from it.

    [Two or three sentences on the approach, described as a problem and a decision, with no internal names.]

    It now handles over a million releases a day. [One sentence on what that taught him.]

    I've written up the details on the blog: [link]

**Not this:**

    🚀 Excited to share that I 10x'd our pipeline throughput! Here are 3 lessons I learned:
    1️⃣ ...
    What would you have done? 👇 #engineering #kafka #leadership

## Instagram: the joy of photography

**What it's for.** Photography for the love of it, and sharing that with other people who love it. The same care for
the craft as everywhere else, but the point is enjoyment.

**What goes on it.** Photos, mostly. Infrared, vintage glass, CampSnap shots through his own filters, the dog, the
garden. Sometimes a thing he's printed. CampSnap filter work shows up here as photos taken through the filters.

**The helping side.** James has spent years helping other photographers get into the hobby: the mistakes he made so
they don't have to, what's worth worrying about, and avoiding gear acquisition syndrome. A caption can carry one of
those tips when the photo suggests it. One tip, said kindly, the way he'd answer a question. Never a lecture.

**How a caption is built.**

- A title or one line about the moment ("Bulldog on his afternoon walk").
- Optionally, one or two sentences on why the shot works or what he was going for.
- Optionally, a gear and settings line, for people who want it: `Helios 44-2 · f/2 · 1/500 · ISO 200`.
- Optionally, one tip.
- A few specific hashtags at the end.

**Length.** Short. One line is often enough. Up to about 80 words when there's a tip.

**Tag me.** CampSnap filter posts may ask people to tag `@jamescodesthings` when they shoot with the filters. Instagram
is the only platform the tag-me ask points to.

**Example: a photo with a tip**

    Country road, shot in infrared through a 550nm filter.

    If you're tempted by infrared, try a cheap filter on a camera you already own before converting a body. Plenty of
    lenses show a hotspot in IR, and it's better to find out which of yours do for the price of a filter.

    #infrared #550nm #countryside

Check the hotspot claim against James's own experience before posting. The tip is an example of the form, and his
own tips are better.

**Example: CampSnap filters**

    One photo, four films. Shot on the CampSnap Pro through the Lab Archive filters, straight off the card.

    The pack is free at codesthings.com/campsnap. If you shoot with them, tag me; I love seeing them out in the world.

    #campsnap #campsnappro

## GitHub: the workshop

**What it's for.** The open workshop. Repos get made, some are half-finished, some are daft ideas, and all of them
are shared so others can use them, learn from them, or pick them up later. It shows the process, including the parts
that didn't work, so people can see that failing is part of building something and work out how to help themselves.

**What goes on it.** Real fixes and real code from his own projects, opened up. Things he did for himself that might
help someone else: mods (Terraria on Nexus, flight sim add-ons, the Motor Town economy guide), configs, tools,
plugins. Instructions for whoever comes after, so they can do it again without him.

**How a README is built.**

1. **What it is**, in one sentence.
2. **Why it exists.** The itch it scratched, in a line or two.
3. **How to use it.** Steps that work on a clean machine.
4. **What's rough.** Known issues, what he tried that didn't work, what he'd do next.
5. **For the next person.** How to keep it going if he's moved on.

**Tone.** The most relaxed of the four. Humour is welcome. The care shows in the instructions: they're complete, and
they're written for someone who has never seen the project.

**Repo descriptions.** One line, says what it does: "A beginner-friendly guide to editing economy values in Motor
Town, and Unreal Engine 5 games in general."

**Example: README opening**

    # Motor Town economy modding guide

    A beginner-friendly guide to editing economy values in Motor Town, and Unreal Engine 5 games in general.

    [The itch, in one sentence: what he wanted to change in the game.] It turned into [how long it took, and what
    it involved], and this guide is what I wish I'd had at the start, including what didn't work.

**Not this:**

    # 🚀 MotorTownEconomyMod
    A powerful, blazing-fast, production-ready economy mod! ⭐ Star if you like it!

## The blog: where depth goes

**What it's for.** Anything that needs more than a few short paragraphs. The blog post comes first. A LinkedIn post
follows two or three days later with the one idea worth taking away and a link.

**Voice.** The same as LinkedIn, with room to explain: the problem, the decisions and why, what went wrong, what
changed, real figures. Headings where they help a reader find their way. Code and diagrams where they show something
words can't.

## When something isn't covered

Ask these in order. The first answer that's clearly a no means a rewrite.

1. Is it true, and can the facts be checked in a file?
2. Would James say this out loud to someone across a table?
3. Does it help or interest the reader, or is it for the algorithm?
4. Does it show the work, or just describe it with adjectives?
5. Will it still read well a year from now?
6. Is it kind, to the reader and to anyone it mentions?

## Before showing James a draft

- [ ] Read it next to the reference post. Does it sound like the same person?
- [ ] Opens with the story; no bait hook; ends on an invitation, not a request for engagement.
- [ ] Every figure and claim traced to a file.
- [ ] No banned phrases, no em dashes, no hashtags on LinkedIn, no emoji on LinkedIn or GitHub.
- [ ] Media attached, with alt text for every image.
- [ ] Standing rules: no phone number; never link the Reddit profile; "senior or principal", never "staff";
  Coventry, UK; nothing confidential from an employer.
- [ ] Two or three drafts when the tone is uncertain, each different in a way that's worth choosing between, with a
  one-line note on how they differ.

## Prompt for any other post

LinkedIn and blog posts in the schedule use the prompt in `linkedin/schedule.md`. For anything else, an Instagram
caption, a README, a repo description or a one-off post, paste this into a Claude Code session started in
`~/projects/codesthings.com`:

    Draft <what: an Instagram caption for <photo>, a README for <repo>, a LinkedIn post about <topic>>.

    Read resources/profiles/voice.md first, including the reference post, and follow it: the voice everywhere,
    the style rules, and the section for this platform. Use only facts you can point to in a file: the repo, the
    photo's metadata, cv/data/*.json or the profile notes under resources/profiles/. Where a fact is missing, leave
    a bracket for me to fill rather than guessing.

    Run the checklist at the end of voice.md before showing me anything. If the tone is uncertain, give me two or
    three drafts that differ in ways worth choosing between, with a line on how they differ.

    Don't post, publish or push anything. Show me the drafts, then wait.
